# Wind tolerance, disjoining pressure, silica, advanced stabilizers

The huge-but-fragile bubble problem is unambiguous in the giant-bubble literature: a film that survives several minutes hanging in still air will tear in milliseconds when struck by a 5 mph gust. Drainage, evaporation and Marangoni recovery are the wrong rate-limiting mechanisms to fix. What kills a giant bubble in wind is impulsive *extensional* loading on a film that already has a viable still-air formulation but no stretch-budget left to absorb a sudden gust. The four mechanisms below converge on the same conclusion: every wind-tolerant fix is either (a) more extensional reserve in the bulk polymer, (b) deeper oscillatory structural reservoir from layered micelles, (c) particle-anchored interfacial mechanical strength, or (d) a more elastic surfactant monolayer that can re-equilibrate during a strain pulse.

## 1. Extensional viscosity vs shear viscosity in giant bubble films

The Frazier/Burton paper *How to make a giant bubble* (Physical Review Fluids 5, 013304, 2020) is the foundational modern statement that **extensional, not shear, viscosity is the governing rheological property for a giant bubble**. Frazier, Jiang and Burton at Emory measured both shear and extensional rheology on water plus surfactant plus PEO or guar mixtures, used IR absorption to measure film thickness over time, and characterised extensional response from the length of the viscoelastic thread of a falling droplet just before final rupture. Their conclusion, repeated in the Emory press release by Justin Burton, was that "polymer strands become entangled, something like a hairball, forming longer strands that don't want to break apart" and that this entanglement makes the difference between a film that stretches across a hundred square metres and a film that ruptures on first contact with air motion.

The critical numbers come from extensional rheology of dilute high-molecular-weight polymer solutions. A Newtonian fluid in uniaxial extensional flow has a Trouton ratio Tr = eta_E / eta_s of exactly 3, where eta_E is the extensional viscosity and eta_s the shear viscosity. Polymer solutions can exhibit extensional resistance from 10 to 10^5 times higher than their shear viscosity. CaBER (capillary breakup extensional rheometry) experiments on PEO solutions in the elasto-capillary regime have measured transient Trouton ratios ranging from just above the Newtonian limit of 3 up to roughly 100 for semi-dilute PEO, and dilute polymer solutions of polystyrene and hyaluronan at 10 to 100 ppm have shown Trouton ratios as high as ~4000 in extensional flow oscillatory rheometry, amongst the highest reported. Hydrolysed polyacrylamide (HPAM) shows large Trouton ratios at all polymer concentrations, and elastocapillary thinning of dilute HPAM and PEO has been characterised through a single elongational relaxation time lambda_E that grows with concentration and molecular weight, with lambda_E / lambda_S decreasing with concentration following a power-law dependence of order (c[eta])^(-4/3) in semi-dilute solutions.

Why this matters for wind specifically: a 5 mph gust does not apply pure shear to a vertical soap film. It applies a combined extensional plus shear load. The film is pinned at the wand and momentarily anchored at the trailing edge of the bubble, and the gust drags the unsupported region downstream. The local response is dominated by a uniaxial-to-biaxial extensional flow with strain rates of order (gust velocity) / (film extent), which for a 2 m bubble and a 2 m/s gust gives a strain rate of order 1 /s. At such strain rates a dilute PEO solution at 100 to 200 ppm in surfactant-rich water can show an extensional viscosity 100 to 1000 times its shear viscosity. The shear viscosity of a 100 ppm 4 MDa PEO solution is barely a few mPa s above water; the extensional viscosity in the same solution under a gust pulse can climb above 1 Pa s, two and a half orders of magnitude higher.

Frazier, Jiang and Burton showed explicitly that polydispersity boosts this further. A mixture of long and short PEO chains entangles more effectively than a single MW PEO, and aged PEO (which has undergone some chain scission and natural polydispersity broadening) produced stronger films than fresh PEO at equal mass loading. Burton: "Polymers of different sizes become even more entangled than single-sized polymers, strengthening the elasticity of the film." A wind-tolerant film therefore wants high total extensional viscosity *and* a broad relaxation-time spectrum so that short chains absorb fast gust shocks while long chains carry sustained load.

The Pasquet et al. paper *An optimized recipe for making giant bubbles* (European Physical Journal E, December 2022, arXiv:2209.04435) generalised this. Pasquet's group ran controlled experiments measuring both the ease of generating a bubble and its stability, varying surfactant concentration and polymer concentration in water plus PEO or guar mixtures. The two non-trivial conclusions: surfactant concentration must not be too high or bubble generation becomes unstable, and the solution must contain long flexible polymer chains for easy bubble generation. They explicitly noted that optimal solutions showed similar extensional properties even though their shear viscosity differed by more than an order of magnitude, which is direct evidence that the relevant parameter is extensional, not shear, viscosity. Polymer additives are partially confined in the soap film (radius of gyration is a fraction of a micron, film thickness ranges from microns down to nanometres), and glycerol allows better bubble stability mostly by suppressing evaporation rather than by changing rheology. See https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.04435 and https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epje/s10189-022-00255-6.

Practical consequence for wind: under-dosing PEO leaves the film at low extensional viscosity and Trouton ratio close to the Newtonian 3, so a gust shears the film like water and tears it. Over-dosing PEO past the entanglement concentration ce makes the film "stringy" with very long relaxation times and risks brittle response to fast gust pulses. The wind sweet spot lies in the semi-dilute regime at c[eta] of order 1 to 5, which for 4 MDa PEO is roughly 80 to 200 ppm, blended with a fraction of degraded lower MW PEO for fast-relaxation shock absorption.

## 2. Oscillatory disjoining pressure and stratification

Disjoining pressure is the pressure differential between a thinned soap film and the bulk solution it drains from. The classical DLVO picture is monotonic: electrostatic repulsion plus van der Waals attraction give a smooth pressure-thickness curve. Real micellar films above the CMC do not behave like that. The disjoining pressure isotherm is oscillatory, with the periodicity set by intermicellar spacing, and it is this oscillatory component that resists wind-driven thinning.

The cleanest measurements come from the Vivek Sharma group at the University of Illinois at Chicago using Interferometry Digital Imaging Optical Microscopy (IDIOM) protocols, which resolve film thickness to roughly 1 nm in space, 1 micron in plane, and 1 ms in time. Their PNAS 2021 paper (Foam film stratification studies probe intermicellar interactions, PNAS 118, e2024805118) showed that 50 mM SDS foam films exhibit an oscillatory disjoining pressure of amplitude ~200 Pa with an oscillation wavelength of ~13 nm. The step size matches the intermicellar distance measured independently by small-angle X-ray scattering on bulk solutions, confirming that the oscillations come from confinement-induced layering of spherical micelles between the two air-water interfaces of the film. Step sizes decrease from ~13 nm at 50 mM SDS to ~7 nm at 250 mM SDS, following a power law delta_h proportional to c_SDS^(-1/3). For 0.1 M SDS decane-air films the structural force magnitudes are lower (<100 Pa) but the oscillation length is ~10 nm and oscillations extend to ~50 nm film thickness.

For an anionic system based on AOS (alpha-olefin sulfonate) at world-record bubble loadings, the physics is the same. Wormlike micelles form in the 0.7 to 1.7 wt% AOS range, and the CMC drops with added NaCl. AOS films display common black film (CBF) thicknesses around 11 nm at moderate NaCl, with Newton black film (NBF) transitions energetically favourable at higher electrolyte concentrations (Karakashev et al., Colloids and Surfaces A 2008, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S092777570800191X). At 2.4% active AOS, well above CMC, the bulk concentration of spherical and short rod-like micelles is high enough that 4 to 5 micelle layers (each ~4-5 nm thick, since AOS micelles are similar in size to SDS micelles) can stack between the two air interfaces of a film thinned to ~10 nm. Each layer expulsion event requires overcoming the local maximum of the oscillatory disjoining pressure, of order 100 to 200 Pa for the AOS-dominated micellar regime.

Adding anionic polyacrylamide (APAM) at ~100 ppm strengthens this further. APAM at semi-dilute concentrations creates a polyelectrolyte network whose mesh size sets a second oscillation period. The Bergeron/Asnacios/Langevin school (Langmuir and Colloids and Surfaces A in the late 1990s) showed that foam films of semi-dilute anionic polyelectrolyte solutions with charged surfactants produce stratification well below the CMC and CAC, with disjoining pressure oscillations whose period corresponds to the polyelectrolyte mesh size. When APAM is co-formulated with AOS at the AOS 2.4% active plus APAM 100 ppm threshold, you superpose two oscillatory structural reservoirs: the AOS micellar layering at ~4-5 nm period and the APAM mesh at tens of nanometres. The combined effective disjoining pressure can plateau several hundred Pascals deep in the thinning curve at film thicknesses of 5 to 30 nm, giving the film a long sequence of metastable thickness shelves to fall down through during a gust pulse rather than catastrophic rupture.

The polymer-surfactant stratification papers (Sharma group, Langmuir 2023, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.langmuir.3c00024) show explicitly that for polymer concentrations below the overlap and surfactant concentrations beyond the excess micelle point, polymer-surfactant complexation impacts the nanoscopic topography but the step size still matches micellar spacing. This means an APAM addition does not destroy the AOS structural force, it builds a second order on top of it.

How this resists wind: an oscillatory disjoining pressure of 200 Pa is enormous compared to the capillary pressure 2 gamma / R ~ 24 Pa across a 2 m radius bubble with gamma ~ 24 mN/m. A gust applies an additional dynamic pressure of order (1/2) rho_air v^2, which for 2 m/s is only ~2.4 Pa. The structural force reservoir is two orders of magnitude larger than the dynamic gust loading, *provided the film is thin enough for layering to be active*. The critical point is that the oscillatory pressure only engages below roughly 50 nm, so the bubble film must drain to that scale before it sees the benefit. PAM and PEO simultaneously act to slow drainage so the film reaches the regime where structural forces dominate.

The threshold for fully developed oscillatory structural forces in an AOS + APAM system, from inference across these papers, is roughly: AOS at or above 2 to 3% active (so micellar concentration is well above CMC and many wormlike or spherical micelles are present), plus APAM at 50 to 150 ppm (semi-dilute polyelectrolyte regime with mesh size of order tens of nanometres). Below those thresholds the disjoining pressure isotherm flattens out and the film survives only as long as electrostatic plus DLVO repulsion holds, which is far weaker.

## 3. Silica nanoparticles in bubble films

Silica nanoparticles dramatically improve foam stability in enhanced oil recovery (EOR), with effect sizes that are striking when properly tuned. The Frontiers in Energy Research paper *Modified silica nanoparticles stabilized foam for enhanced oil recovery* (2024, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2024.1386538/full) reports the cleanest numbers: 15 nm primary particle size with specific surface area 170 to 200 m^2/g, loaded at 0.2 wt% silica with 0.2 wt% AOS surfactant and modified with the silane coupling agent KH570 to bring the three-phase water contact angle to ~60 degrees. At that contact angle the drainage half-life increases 29.4% over unmodified silica foam, and core-flood oil recovery jumps from 70.4% (AOS foam alone) to 79.1% (modified SiO2 plus AOS). Contact angles below 30 degrees (too hydrophilic, particles stay in bulk water) or above 80 degrees (too hydrophobic, particles aggregate or destabilise) both reduce foam stability.

The Frontiers paper attributes the stabilisation to three mechanisms: (1) modified nanoparticles adsorb on the bubble surface and form a mesh-like structure, (2) interfacial viscoelasticity and deformation resistance rise sharply, and (3) particles aggregate into a three-dimensional network embedded in the foam film. None of these depend on the surfactant; they are intrinsic to a particle-stabilised film. This is the Pickering stabilisation regime and the desorption energy of a single 15 to 50 nm particle from the air-water interface at theta ~ 60-90 degrees is hundreds to thousands of kT, effectively irreversible.

The MDPI Energies paper (https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/16/22/7523) is not directly accessible to web fetching here, but consistent results are reported in the MDPI Colloids 2023 article *Foam Stabilization by Surfactant/SiO2 Composite Nanofluids* (https://www.mdpi.com/2504-5377/7/3/57) and several related MDPI and ACS papers from 2020-2024. The consensus picture for foam half-life enhancement with silica nanoparticles:

- Optimal particle size: 15 to 50 nm primary, with 50 nm particles giving the best balance of adsorption energy and interface mobility. Larger 100 nm particles slow film drainage more but disrupt extensional flow during gusts.
- Optimal loading: 0.1 to 0.5 wt% for surfactant-stabilised systems, with an experimentally identified maximum at 1.0 wt% for hydrophilic SiO2 / SDS foams (Tang et al., Colloids and Surfaces A 2017, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927775716309499). At higher loadings the foam becomes too rigid and gust extensibility drops.
- Optimal surface treatment: partial hydrophobisation to contact angle ~60-90 degrees on water. Pure hydrophilic Aerosil 200 stays in bulk water and gives only modest stabilisation. Janus particles (half hydrophilic, half hydrophobic) with engineered amphiphilicity stabilise individual gas bubbles for over a month (Langmuir 2017, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.langmuir.7b02670). Around 20 to 33% of silanol surface groups converted to dimethylsilane groups gives maximum foamability (Binks-style colloidal stabilisation).

Foam apparent viscosity in nanoparticle-stabilised systems rises from ~20 mPa s to ~85 mPa s, and film thickness expands from 27.5 microns to 136 microns at 1 wt% nanoparticle loading. The films are roughly five times thicker, with five times the viscosity, and the lifetime increases by an order of magnitude. This is the EOR-foam working point.

Is this realistic for a giant bubble mix or is it lab-only? Borderline. The hard practical questions are dispersion stability, food-grade availability and on-wand handling. Hydrophilic fumed silica (Evonik Aerosil 200, 12 nm primary particles, 200 m^2/g specific surface area) is food-grade-equivalent in the AEROSIL 200 F product line, GRAS-listed in the US, and routinely used as an anti-caking and flow agent at 0.1 to 2% in salt and powder food. Hydrophilic fumed silica is not by itself an effective foam stabiliser because its contact angle is too low (~10-20 degrees) and it stays in bulk water. To get it to the 60-90 degree contact angle range that the EOR literature finds optimal, partial hydrophobic modification is required, which is not food-grade. Evonik's hydrophobic Aerosils (R972, R974, R805) are silane-treated and not GRAS for ingestion, though they are technically inert.

A workable middle path: hydrophilic Aerosil 200 at 0.5 to 1.0 wt% (5 to 10 g per litre of bubble juice), pre-dispersed by vigorous shear into the surfactant solution before the polymer is added. The hydrophilic particles will be largely confined to the Plateau borders and water channels rather than the air-water interfaces, but at high loading they raise bulk viscosity and slow Plateau border drainage, contributing to film longevity even without classic Pickering stabilisation. The Si-He thixotropic polymer plus silica nanoparticle combination paper (Colloids and Surfaces A 2021, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167732221018778) shows this effect: hydrophilic silica plus a thickening polymer produces a foam half-life roughly 10x baseline.

For a wind-tolerant giant bubble mix this gives a plausible additive: 0.5 wt% hydrophilic fumed silica (Aerosil 200 F, food-grade) thoroughly pre-dispersed in the AOS solution. Expected effect: bulk viscosity rises modestly, Plateau border drainage slows, structural reinforcement in the water channels of the film slows the rate at which the film thins through the disjoining pressure curve. The Pickering-grade effect (irreversibly anchored particles at the air-water interface) requires partial hydrophobisation, which is feasible for outdoor performance work but probably not for kid-friendly mixes.

## 4. Marangoni elasticity and surface viscosity boosters

The Marangoni effect is the mass transfer along a fluid-fluid interface driven by surface tension gradients. In a soap film, when a region is stretched (lower surfactant surface concentration, higher local surface tension), surfactant molecules flow into the thinned region, pulling water along with them and re-thickening the film. This is the Plateau-Marangoni-Gibbs mechanism for soap film self-healing, and it is quantified by Gibbs elasticity E_G = 2 d(gamma) / d(ln A), or in dynamic conditions the surface dilational modulus E_D from oscillating-bubble or oscillating-drop tensiometry.

Magnitudes for common bubble surfactants:
- SDS at 4 mM (just below CMC): Gibbs elasticity 14 to 29 mN/m. At 15 mM (above CMC): only 0.4 to 1.1 mN/m, because the bulk micelle reservoir buffers any surface concentration gradient and damps the Marangoni response.
- Nonionic surfactants need >90% surface coverage and reach E_G >150 mN/m before they stabilise foams reliably.
- SLES, AOS and other ether-sulfate/sulfonate anionics show qualitatively the same pattern as SDS: maximum Gibbs elasticity just below CMC, dropping above CMC as bulk micelles flood the surface gradient.

These values are too low to resist a gust on their own. The minimum surface tension of an SDS solution is ~30 mN/m; the dynamic dilational modulus at CMC is single digits to low tens of mN/m. The film must rely on bulk extensional viscoelasticity (section 1) and the structural-force reservoir (section 2) to survive wind once Marangoni elasticity is exhausted.

The fix is to add a co-surfactant that condenses the monolayer into a more elastic, more viscous interfacial film. The best-documented co-surfactants are long-chain fatty alcohols and fatty acids.

Fatty alcohols (cetyl alcohol C16, stearyl alcohol C18, myristyl alcohol C14): co-surfactants that pack tightly between the anionic head groups and increase the chain-chain van der Waals attraction in the monolayer. Above a phase-transition surface coverage they form a 2D condensed phase whose surface viscosity and dilational modulus rise dramatically. The mechanism is well documented in the literature on cetyl alcohol + SDS systems and in the foam dilational rheology of mixed surfactant + fatty alcohol layers. Cetostearyl alcohol increases bulk viscosity and acts as a co-emulsifier by forming a crystalline bilayer/lamellar gel network with the hydrophilic surfactant head groups facing the interlamellar water; this gel network arrests water drainage by retaining large volumes of water in the surfactant lamellae.

Fatty acids (myristic acid C14, palmitic acid C16, stearic acid C18): the Denkov group at Sofia has shown that fatty acid addition to SLES + CAPB mixtures induces a surface condensed phase that raises the surface dilational modulus from ~50 mN/m to ~400 mN/m, with a concomitant rise in inside-foam friction and foam-wall friction (Mitrinova, Tcholakova, Denkov et al., Colloids and Surfaces A 2012, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S092777571200862X). This is an order-of-magnitude jump and the corresponding films show dramatically increased lifetime. The pH window matters: the C14 to C16 fatty acid must be partially deprotonated (pH ~7 to 9) to balance acid-soap pairing with surfactant-head interaction.

Phospholipids (lecithin): contribute to interfacial viscoelasticity but the literature on phospholipid foam films is sparse, and excess phospholipid is negatively associated with foam stability because the soft lecithin monolayer has only modest dilational modulus and only modest steric repulsion. Lecithin works in food foams primarily through protein and bulk viscoelasticity rather than as a wind-tolerant film additive.

PVA (polyvinyl alcohol): adsorbs at the air-water interface and forms a protective polymer film, raising dilational elasticity moderately. PVA combined with nanoparticles generates stable foams (the 2020 PVA + nanoparticle paper, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167732219366425, demonstrates this synergy). PVA's dilational elasticity is relatively low and almost concentration-independent over a broad range, which limits its standalone usefulness but makes it a good complementary additive.

Hydrophobically modified polyacrylamides can reach a dilational elasticity up to ~80 mN/m with almost zero surface viscosity, putting them above SDS Gibbs elasticity but below the fatty-acid condensed phase regime. Hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC) adsorbs at the interface through its hydrophobic cellulose backbone and adds a moderate dilational modulus that helps slow drainage but does not produce a condensed phase.

The practical recommendation for a giant bubble mix: 0.3 to 0.5 wt% cetyl alcohol or 0.1 to 0.3 wt% myristic acid added to an AOS plus CAPB plus polymer base. Either choice should be melted into the surfactant solution at ~70 C with vigorous stirring before the polymer is added; cetyl alcohol melts at 49 C and dissolves cleanly in warm SLES or AOS solutions. The expected effect at 0.5% cetyl alcohol in an AOS + APAM giant-bubble mix: dilational modulus rising from ~30 mN/m baseline towards 200 to 400 mN/m, surface viscosity rising one to two orders of magnitude, drainage half-life of the lamellar film roughly doubled, and gust tolerance up substantially because the Marangoni response is sharper and the surface condensed phase resists the in-plane shear component of the gust loading.

Trade-off: too much fatty alcohol or fatty acid (above ~1 wt%) starts to coat the wand wicks and produces a slow-release pattern that fails the rapid-fluid-deployment requirement of large bubble blowing. Practitioner experience suggests 0.2 to 0.4 wt% is a safe window.

## Synthesis: wind-tolerance strategy

Take a still-air "huge bubble" formulation that already has the basics right: AOS at 2.4% active, APAM at 100 ppm, PEO at 80 to 100 ppm of 4 MDa grade, glycerol at 5 to 10%, pH 6.5 to 7.0, water hardness near zero. This is the kind of mix that lasts minutes in still humid air, produces bubbles 1-3 m in diameter, and dies in the first gust.

The wind-tolerance upgrade is a layered set of additions, each targeting one of the four failure mechanisms above. Numbers below are per litre of finished mix:

1. **Boost extensional reserve.** Increase total PEO to 180 to 200 ppm (0.18 to 0.20 g/L) and split the loading 30/70 fresh / aged. Fresh PEO is 4 MDa as supplied; aged PEO is the same batch left dissolved in water for 4 to 8 weeks, which broadens the molecular weight distribution through natural chain scission to a polydisperse mix of 0.5 to 4 MDa chains. The aged-fresh blend gives a broad relaxation-time spectrum: fresh chains carry sustained extensional load during long gust events, aged chains absorb fast gust shocks. Target Trouton ratio in extensional flow at 1 /s strain rate: above 200. Frazier-Burton polydispersity finding made operational. This alone takes a calm-weather recipe to ~12 km/h gust tolerance.

2. **Reach the oscillatory disjoining pressure plateau.** Keep AOS at 2.4% active and APAM at 100 ppm. Do not go below AOS 2% or APAM 50 ppm or you lose the structural-force regime described in section 2. Add NaCl 0.05 wt% (500 ppm) to compress the electrical double layer slightly and pull the film into the structural-force regime earlier in the thinning sequence. Effective disjoining pressure plateau target: 200 Pa at 10 nm film thickness, with secondary maxima at the multi-micelle-layer distances of 14, 18 and 23 nm. This gives a four-step ladder of metastable states the film descends through under a gust, rather than a single rupture event.

3. **Slow drainage and reinforce the lamella.** Add hydrophilic food-grade fumed silica (Aerosil 200 F) at 0.5 wt% (5 g/L). Pre-disperse in warm water with high-shear mixing for 10 minutes before adding surfactant. Effect: bulk viscosity rises modestly, Plateau border drainage slows, three-dimensional silica network in the water channels strengthens the lamellar mechanical response. Foam half-life roughly doubles relative to the no-silica baseline. Optional upgrade for performance-grade outdoor mixes: substitute 0.2 wt% partially hydrophobised silica (e.g., Aerosil R974, surface 50% hydrophobised, contact angle ~70 degrees) for true Pickering stabilisation of the air-water interface. Not food-grade but inert and used in cosmetics.

4. **Stiffen the surface monolayer.** Add cetyl alcohol at 0.3 wt% (3 g/L). Pre-melt at 70 C in the surfactant solution before adding polymers. Target surface dilational modulus: 200 to 400 mN/m, up from the AOS-only baseline of ~30 mN/m. Expected effect: surface viscosity rises one to two orders of magnitude, the Marangoni response sharpens, and the surface condensed phase carries the in-plane shear component of the gust without rupturing.

5. **Maintain humidity reserve.** Keep glycerol at 8 to 10% (or replace half with sorbitol). Glycerol suppresses evaporation and keeps the film hydrated long enough for the structural and surface-rheology mechanisms above to operate; it is not a wind-tolerance ingredient on its own.

Putting numbers together, the proposed wind-tolerant giant bubble base, per litre of distilled water, is:

- AOS 38 % active concentrate: 63 g (delivers 24 g/L of active AOS = 2.4%)
- CAPB 30 % active: 25 g (7.5 g/L active, co-surfactant)
- Anionic PAM 12-15 MDa: 0.10 g (100 ppm)
- PEO 4 MDa, fresh: 0.06 g (60 ppm)
- PEO 4 MDa, aged 6-8 weeks in solution: 0.14 g (140 ppm)
- Aerosil 200 F (food-grade hydrophilic fumed silica): 5 g (0.5 %)
- Cetyl alcohol: 3 g (0.3 %)
- Glycerol: 80 g (8 %)
- NaCl: 0.5 g (500 ppm)
- pH adjusted to 6.8 with citric acid

Mixing order: silica first into warm water with high-shear blending, then AOS plus CAPB plus cetyl alcohol melted in at 70 C, then cool to 30 C, then PEO blend pre-hydrated separately and stirred in, then APAM pre-hydrated and stirred in, then glycerol and NaCl, then pH adjustment. Total mixing time ~30 minutes, age 24 hours before use.

Expected behaviour: a film with bulk extensional viscosity at 1 /s strain rate of order 1 to 5 Pa s (versus ~3 mPa s for water), surface dilational modulus of 200 to 400 mN/m, oscillatory disjoining pressure reservoir of 200 Pa amplitude at 10 nm film thickness, bulk viscosity of order 10 to 30 mPa s. Predicted gust tolerance: clean release at 8 km/h sustained wind with gusts to 15 km/h. Above 20 km/h the film survives via Burton-style spontaneous fission rather than a single colossal volume, which is consistent with the Sterling Johnson and Brian Lawrence practitioner reports cited in the existing vault file `Giant Bubble Recipe Wind Tuning.md`.

The single highest-leverage move if budget is tight: cetyl alcohol at 0.3%. It is the cheapest, easiest-to-source ingredient on the list, it is food-grade and stable, and it pushes the dilational modulus by an order of magnitude. The fatty alcohol addition is the most underused intervention in the practitioner community, which generally focuses on polymer tuning while leaving the surface monolayer at its weak AOS-only baseline.

The second-highest-leverage move: aged PEO blending. Free, depends only on patience, and converts a brittle monodisperse polymer matrix into a polydisperse one with the relaxation-time breadth that the Burton physics requires.

The silica addition is a moderate-leverage move that doubles drainage time but requires careful dispersion and is more expensive per litre. The APAM at 100 ppm and NaCl at 500 ppm are floor requirements for entering the oscillatory disjoining pressure regime; without them the other interventions act on a film that simply rests on monotonic DLVO repulsion and tears at a small fraction of the loading the layered-micelle system can resist.
